# Mathematical Object Origin Archive | Verma Module

## 1. Archive Information

- Standard Name: Verma module
- Mathematical Field: Representation Theory; Lie Groups and Lie Algebras
- Abstract: A Verma module is the universal highest-weight module of a complex semisimple Lie algebra. It is formed by inducing a one-dimensional representation of a Borel subalgebra and turns the construction and classification of highest-weight representations into a concrete problem about submodules and singular vectors.

## 2. Core Record

### Precise Description

Let \(\mathfrak g\) be a finite-dimensional complex semisimple Lie algebra with a triangular decomposition
\[
\mathfrak g=\mathfrak n^-\oplus\mathfrak h\oplus\mathfrak n^+,
\]
and let \(\mathfrak b=\mathfrak h\oplus\mathfrak n^+\) be the corresponding Borel subalgebra. For \(\lambda\in\mathfrak h^*\), define the one-dimensional \(\mathfrak b\)-module \(\mathbb C_\lambda\) by
\[
h\cdot 1=\lambda(h),\qquad x\cdot 1=0
\quad(h\in\mathfrak h,\ x\in\mathfrak n^+).
\]
The Verma module of highest weight \(\lambda\) is
\[
M(\lambda)=U(\mathfrak g)\otimes_{U(\mathfrak b)}\mathbb C_\lambda.
\]
Equivalently, \(M(\lambda)=U(\mathfrak g)/I_\lambda\), where \(I_\lambda\) is the left ideal generated by all \(x\in\mathfrak n^+\) and all \(h-\lambda(h)1\) with \(h\in\mathfrak h\). Its distinguished generator \(v_\lambda=1\otimes1\) has weight \(\lambda\) and is annihilated by \(\mathfrak n^+\). The Poincaré–Birkhoff–Witt theorem gives a vector-space isomorphism
\[
U(\mathfrak n^-)\xrightarrow{\sim}M(\lambda),\qquad u\mapsto u v_\lambda,
\]
so the weights lie in \(\lambda-Q^+\) and
\[
\operatorname{ch}M(\lambda)=e^\lambda\prod_{\alpha\in\Phi^+}(1-e^{-\alpha})^{-1}.
\]
It is universal in the following sense: if a \(\mathfrak g\)-module is generated by a vector \(v\) of weight \(\lambda\) annihilated by \(\mathfrak n^+\), then there is a unique surjective homomorphism \(M(\lambda)\to U(\mathfrak g)v\) sending \(v_\lambda\) to \(v\). Moreover, \(M(\lambda)\) has a unique maximal proper submodule, and its quotient \(L(\lambda)\) is the unique simple highest-weight module of highest weight \(\lambda\) [2].

### Mathematical Context and Formation

The concrete problem class is to construct and analyze irreducible highest-weight representations of a complex semisimple Lie algebra from prescribed highest-weight data. Given \(\lambda\), the relations
\[
\mathfrak n^+v=0,\qquad hv=\lambda(h)v
\]
identify a prospective highest-weight vector, but they do not by themselves display all relations among vectors obtained by repeatedly applying lowering operators. Those additional relations govern reducibility, embeddings, composition factors, and, in the dominant-integral case, the finite-dimensional irreducible representation. Starting directly with an irreducible module hides these relations because the quotient defining irreducibility has already imposed them; finite-dimensional highest-weight theory additionally imposes integrability and therefore is not a universal setting for studying which relations are forced by \(\lambda\).

The decisive construction is to impose only the two highest-weight relations and no others. The one-dimensional \(\mathfrak b\)-module \(\mathbb C_\lambda\) packages those relations, and induction from \(\mathfrak b\) to \(\mathfrak g\) produces \(M(\lambda)\). PBW then separates the lowering algebra \(U(\mathfrak n^-)\) from the prescribed action of \(\mathfrak b\), yielding an explicit ambient module in which every further relation appears as a submodule relation. Verma's original paper studies precisely the structure and homomorphisms of these induced representations, including how extreme (highest-weight) vectors generate subrepresentations [1]. The object now called the Verma module is thus not merely an arbitrary infinite-dimensional representation: its form is dictated by the problem of retaining all consequences of a chosen highest weight before passing to any irreducible or finite-dimensional quotient.

### Essential Role

The Verma module makes the previously hidden quotient data into the main tractable object. Its universal property ensures that every highest-weight module of highest weight \(\lambda\) is a quotient of the same canonical module \(M(\lambda)\); consequently, construction and comparison of such modules reduce to finding submodules of \(M(\lambda)\). The unique maximal proper submodule isolates the irreducible answer canonically as \(L(\lambda)\), rather than requiring that irreducibility be built into the construction from the outset.

Two structural features explain this effectiveness. First, the PBW identification with \(U(\mathfrak n^-)\) provides explicit weight spaces and a known formal character before any quotient is taken. Second, a vector in \(M(\lambda)\) annihilated by \(\mathfrak n^+\) is itself a highest-weight, or singular, vector; by universality it induces a homomorphism from another Verma module. Thus possible submodules and homomorphisms can be detected through concrete highest-weight equations inside a fixed PBW model. This reformulates the hard part of the motivating representation problem—determining extra lowering-operator relations—as the detection of singular vectors and the submodules they generate. In particular, for dominant integral \(\lambda\), the quotient \(L(\lambda)\) is finite-dimensional, and the finite-dimensional irreducible module is recovered from the universal one by imposing exactly the maximal set of proper relations [2].

## 3. Notes

A Verma module should not be confused with its irreducible quotient \(L(\lambda)\). Except in special cases, \(M(\lambda)\) is infinite-dimensional, and it need not be simple. Generalized Verma modules replace the Borel subalgebra by a parabolic subalgebra and may induce from a higher-dimensional module; they are related objects, not alternative names for the object archived here.
