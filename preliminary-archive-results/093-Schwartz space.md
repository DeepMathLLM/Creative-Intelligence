# Mathematical Object Origin Archive | Schwartz Space

## 1. Archive Information

- Standard Name: Schwartz space \(\mathcal S(\mathbb R^n)\)
- Mathematical Field: Harmonic Analysis; Functional Analysis
- Abstract: The Schwartz space is the Fréchet space of smooth functions whose derivatives decay faster than every inverse polynomial. It was formed to provide a test-function space on which the Fourier transform is closed and continuous, thereby making Fourier transformation of an important class of distributions rigorous by duality.

## 2. Core Record

### Precise Description

For multi-indices \(\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb N_0^n\), define
\[
p_{\alpha,\beta}(\varphi)=\sup_{x\in\mathbb R^n}\left|x^\alpha\partial^\beta\varphi(x)\right|.
\]
The Schwartz space is
\[
\mathcal S(\mathbb R^n)
 =\left\{\varphi\in C^\infty(\mathbb R^n):
 p_{\alpha,\beta}(\varphi)<\infty\text{ for every }\alpha,\beta\right\}.
\]
Thus every derivative of \(\varphi\) decreases at infinity faster than any prescribed inverse power of \(|x|\). The locally convex topology generated by the seminorms \(p_{\alpha,\beta}\) makes \(\mathcal S(\mathbb R^n)\) a Fréchet space. With the convention
\[
\widehat\varphi(\xi)=\int_{\mathbb R^n}e^{-ix\cdot\xi}\varphi(x)\,dx,
\]
the Fourier transform is a continuous linear automorphism of \(\mathcal S(\mathbb R^n)\) [2,3]. Its continuous dual \(\mathcal S'(\mathbb R^n)\) is the space of tempered distributions.

### Mathematical Context and Formation

The motivating problem was to extend Fourier transformation from suitably integrable functions to generalized functions used in linear partial differential equations—for example, the Dirac mass, polynomially growing functions, and distributional derivatives. General distributions were already represented as continuous linear functionals on
\(\mathcal D(\mathbb R^n)=C_c^\infty(\mathbb R^n)\). One would like to define their Fourier transforms by transposition,
\[
\langle \widehat T,\varphi\rangle=\langle T,\widehat\varphi\rangle
\]
(up to the normalization and sign convention). The exact obstruction is that \(\widehat\varphi\) is generally not compactly supported when \(\varphi\in\mathcal D\). Hence \(T\in\mathcal D'\), whose domain is only \(\mathcal D\), cannot in general be evaluated on \(\widehat\varphi\); the test-function space used for local distribution theory is not closed under the Fourier transform [1,3]. At the opposite extreme, smooth functions with no decay condition need not have convergent Fourier integrals, while ordinary \(L^1\) or \(L^2\) frameworks do not by themselves supply a test-function dual containing such singular objects as point masses together with the desired differentiation rules.

The needed replacement had to balance smoothness, decay, and Fourier symmetry. Fourier transformation interchanges differentiation and multiplication by coordinates:
\[
\widehat{\partial^\beta\varphi}(\xi)=(i\xi)^\beta\widehat\varphi(\xi),
\qquad
\widehat{x^\alpha\varphi}(\xi)=i^{|\alpha|}\partial_\xi^\alpha\widehat\varphi(\xi)
\]
for the convention above. Requiring every \(x^\alpha\partial^\beta\varphi\) to be bounded therefore controls precisely the two families of operations exchanged by the transform. This produces a space large enough to contain non-compactly-supported functions such as Gaussians, but sufficiently rapidly decreasing that integration by parts and differentiation under the Fourier integral preserve all defining bounds. In this mathematical sense, the seminorm system defining \(\mathcal S\) is the structural answer to the failure of \(C_c^\infty\) to be Fourier-invariant. Schwartz's treatment joined this space to distribution theory and Fourier transformation [1,2].

### Essential Role

The direct achievement of \(\mathcal S(\mathbb R^n)\) is closure of the test-function side of the transposition procedure. Because
\(\mathcal F:\mathcal S\to\mathcal S\) is a continuous automorphism, every tempered distribution \(T\in\mathcal S'\) has a Fourier transform defined without evaluating \(T\) outside its domain, for instance by
\[
\langle \widehat T,\varphi\rangle=\langle T,\widehat\varphi\rangle
\]
with the corresponding convention for the transpose. The result is again in \(\mathcal S'\). The polynomial weights in the seminorms permit the dual to include functions of at most polynomial growth, while the derivative seminorms make distributional differentiation and multiplication by polynomials continuous; point masses and their derivatives also act continuously on \(\mathcal S\) [2,3].

Consequently, the Fourier conversion of constant-coefficient differential operators into polynomial multipliers becomes valid for singular or nonintegrable data within one stable dual space:
\[
P(D)T=f
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
P(i\xi)\widehat T=\widehat f
\]
whenever the terms are interpreted in \(\mathcal S'\) and the stated convention is used. The Schwartz space does not solve division by \(P(i\xi)\) automatically, but it makes that transformed equation meaningful and separates the algebraic multiplier issue from the prior domain failure. Its deeper structural contribution is therefore a Fourier-invariant dual pairing: instead of forcing every generalized function to possess a classical Fourier integral, it transfers the transform to generalized functions through the continuity and invariance of a deliberately chosen test-function space.

## 3. Notes

The Schwartz space \(\mathcal S\) is not the same object as a Schwartz distribution. The latter terminology refers to a continuous linear functional on \(C_c^\infty\), whereas \(\mathcal S\) is the test-function space itself; its dual consists only of tempered distributions, a proper subclass of all distributions. Also, “Schwartz space” can denote a broader class of locally convex spaces in functional analysis, but this archive concerns the canonical rapidly decreasing function space on \(\mathbb R^n\).
