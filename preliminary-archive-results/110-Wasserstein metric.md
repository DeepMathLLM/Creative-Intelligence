# Mathematical Object Origin Archive | Wasserstein metric

## 1. Archive Information

- Standard Name: Wasserstein metric
- Mathematical Field: Probability Theory and Stochastic Processes; optimal transport
- Abstract: The Wasserstein metric measures the separation of two probability measures by the least average ground-space cost of coupling their mass. It formed from the relaxation of the mass-transport problem from deterministic transport maps to joint measures with prescribed marginals, thereby permitting mass splitting and turning a transport optimum into a geometric distance between distributions.

## 2. Core Record

### Precise Description

Let \((X,d)\) be a Polish metric space and let \(p\ge 1\). Write
\[
\mathcal P_p(X)=\left\{\mu\text{ a Borel probability measure on }X:
\int_X d(x,x_0)^p\,d\mu(x)<\infty\right\},
\]
where the condition is independent of the choice of \(x_0\in X\). For \(\mu,\nu\in\mathcal P_p(X)\), let \(\Pi(\mu,\nu)\) denote the probability measures \(\pi\) on \(X\times X\) whose first and second marginals are \(\mu\) and \(\nu\). The \(p\)-Wasserstein metric is
\[
W_p(\mu,\nu)=
\left(\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}
\int_{X\times X} d(x,y)^p\,d\pi(x,y)\right)^{1/p}.
\]
Equivalently, the infimum is over all jointly defined random variables \((U,V)\) with laws \(\mu\) and \(\nu\), of \(\bigl(\mathbb E[d(U,V)^p]\bigr)^{1/p}\). On \(\mathcal P_p(X)\), \(W_p\) is a genuine metric. In the case \(p=1\), Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality gives
\[
W_1(\mu,\nu)=\sup_{\operatorname{Lip}(f)\le 1}
\int_X f\,d(\mu-\nu),
\]
with the integrals well-defined after fixing an additive normalization if necessary [3].

### Mathematical Context and Formation

The motivating problem class is the transportation of one prescribed mass distribution \(\mu\) into another \(\nu\) at minimum cost. In Monge's map formulation, one seeks a measurable map \(T:X\to X\) satisfying \(T_{\#}\mu=\nu\) and minimizes
\[
\int_X c(x,T(x))\,d\mu(x).
\]
The central obstruction is that a deterministic map cannot split the mass at one source point. For example, no map can transport \(\delta_0\) to \(\tfrac12\delta_{-1}+\tfrac12\delta_1\). Thus the admissible set may be empty even though moving the source distribution into the target distribution has an evident probabilistic meaning. Moreover, the marginals \(\mu\) and \(\nu\) do not themselves specify which source mass is paired with which target mass, while that pairing is exactly what determines transportation cost.

Kantorovich's decisive relaxation replaced a single-valued map by a transport plan \(\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)\), so \(\pi(A\times B)\) records how much mass is sent from \(A\) to \(B\). Plans can split an atom, \(\Pi(\mu,\nu)\) is never empty because it contains \(\mu\otimes\nu\), and the objective \(\int c\,d\pi\) is linear on this convex feasible set [1,3]. When the cost is generated by the underlying metric, \(c(x,y)=d(x,y)^p\), the \(p\)-th root of the relaxed optimum is the object now called the Wasserstein metric. The \(p=1\) form had already been investigated by Kantorovich in connection with transport; Vaserstein used the coupling metric in the study of Markov processes on countable product spaces, after which the Wasserstein/Vasershtein name became standard in probability [2,4]. This attribution is therefore layered: the present name recalls Vaserstein, while the transport-plan construction and its metric form have earlier Kantorovich roots.

### Essential Role

The metric made the previously defective part of the transport problem—the need to choose a feasible, cost-sensitive matching of two arbitrary distributions—tractable by optimizing over couplings. In the atomic example above, the plan
\[
\pi=\tfrac12\delta_{(0,-1)}+\tfrac12\delta_{(0,1)}
\]
is admissible although no Monge map is. More generally, under the standard Polish-space and lower-semicontinuity hypotheses, compactness/tightness arguments yield existence of optimal plans for the cost \(d^p\), so relaxation is not merely a formal infimum [3].

Specific features of the definition produce the change. The marginal constraints preserve exactly the prescribed source and target distributions; allowing every joint law supplies the missing freedom to split and pair mass; and integrating \(d(x,y)^p\) retains the geometry and scale of actual displacement. Taking the infimum removes dependence on an arbitrary pairing. Finally, the \(p\)-th root and the metric structure of \(d\) make the optimum itself a distance: symmetry comes from reversing a coupling, identity of indiscernibles from zero cost forcing concentration on the diagonal, and the triangle inequality from gluing couplings through an intermediate marginal and applying Minkowski's inequality [3]. Thus the object did more than assign an optimal value: it reformulated mass transportation as geometry on \(\mathcal P_p(X)\), with \(W_p(\delta_x,\delta_y)=d(x,y)\), so the geometry of the original space is retained inside the space of distributions.

## 3. Notes

The Wasserstein metric is the optimal value associated with the special costs \(d^p\), not a synonym for an individual optimal transport plan and not for the entire subject of optimal transport. “Kantorovich metric” is often used for \(W_1\); “Kantorovich–Rubinstein metric” usually emphasizes its Lipschitz dual representation. Without finite \(p\)-th moments the same formula may be infinite, so it does not define a finite metric on all probability measures.
