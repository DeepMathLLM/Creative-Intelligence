# Mathematical Object Origin Archive | Coxeter group

## 1. Archive Information

- Standard Name: Coxeter group
- Mathematical Field: Abstract Algebra; discrete geometry
- Abstract: A Coxeter group is a group generated by involutions whose defining relations prescribe the orders of pairwise products. It arose from the problem of describing and classifying discrete groups generated by geometric reflections, especially the symmetry groups of regular polytopes and the reflection groups associated with root systems. Its presentation converts the geometry of mirrors and their dihedral angles into a Coxeter matrix or diagram, making a geometric classification problem accessible to algebraic and combinatorial analysis.

## 2. Core Record

### Precise Description

A **Coxeter system** is a pair \((W,S)\) in which \(W\) has the presentation
\[
W=\left\langle S\ \middle|\ (st)^{m_{st}}=1\text{ whenever }m_{st}<\infty\right\rangle .
\]
Here the **Coxeter matrix** \(M=(m_{st})_{s,t\in S}\) is symmetric, \(m_{ss}=1\), and
\[
m_{st}\in\{2,3,4,\ldots\}\cup\{\infty\}\qquad(s\ne t).
\]
The convention \(m_{st}=\infty\) means that no relation on the order of \(st\) is imposed. Since \(m_{ss}=1\), every generator satisfies \(s^2=1\). A group admitting such a generating set is a **Coxeter group**. Strictly, the matrix and diagram belong to the system \((W,S)\), since the same abstract group can admit different Coxeter generating sets [3].

The Coxeter diagram records the same data: its vertices are the elements of \(S\); an edge joins \(s\) and \(t\) when \(m_{st}\ge 3\), conventionally with no label for \(m_{st}=3\) and with label \(m_{st}\) for \(m_{st}\ge4\), including \(\infty\). Omitting edges with \(m_{st}=2\) records that the corresponding generators commute.

This presentation abstracts a basic geometric calculation. If \(s\) and \(t\) are reflections in hyperplanes meeting at angle \(\pi/m\), then \(st\) is a rotation through \(2\pi/m\), so \((st)^m=1\). The abstraction retains these pairwise angular orders without requiring an initially specified Euclidean realization. Conversely, the standard geometric representation puts a real vector space with basis \(\{\alpha_s:s\in S\}\) under the symmetric form
\[
B(\alpha_s,\alpha_t)=-\cos(\pi/m_{st}),
\]
where \(\cos(\pi/\infty)=1\), and represents \(s\) by
\[
v\longmapsto v-2B(v,\alpha_s)\alpha_s.
\]
This representation is faithful; it realizes the abstract generators as linear reflections, although for an infinite Coxeter group the form need not be positive definite [3].

### Mathematical Context and Formation

The motivating problem class was to determine the discrete groups generated by reflections in the faces of a fundamental region in spherical or Euclidean space, and in particular to classify their finite cases. This included symmetry groups of regular polytopes, whose fundamental chambers are spherical simplices, and Weyl groups obtained from root systems. These examples were geometrically different in origin, but each was generated by reflections and each chamber carried integer angle data of the form \(\pi/m_{st}\) [1][4].

The difficulty was not merely to list familiar symmetries. A realization by matrices, a constructed polytope, or a root system described one group in one ambient space, but did not by itself expose a common finite datum from which one could recover the group, decide finiteness, and compare examples arising in different dimensions. Moreover, the obvious relations—each reflection is an involution and two wall reflections have product of order determined by their angle—had to be shown sufficient: without sufficiency, the angle data could give only relations satisfied by the geometric group rather than a presentation of it.

The decisive formation was to take the reflections in the walls of a chamber as distinguished generators and promote their pairwise angular relations to the defining presentation
\((st)^{m_{st}}=1\). Thus a configuration of mirrors became the matrix \((m_{st})\), and then a labeled graph. Coxeter's study of discrete groups generated by reflections established this presentation framework for the geometric groups [1], while the ensuing enumeration treated finite groups directly in the form \(r_i^2=(r_ir_j)^{k_{ij}}=1\) [2]. In modern terms, the associated bilinear form with entries \(-\cos(\pi/m_{st})\) supplies the bridge back from the abstract presentation to reflection geometry. For finite-rank systems, positive definiteness of this form characterizes finiteness, turning the finite classification into the classification of the corresponding positive-definite diagrams [3].

### Essential Role

The Coxeter group made tractable the step from local chamber geometry to the global reflection group. Its involutory generators preserve the individual mirrors algebraically, while the exponents \(m_{st}\) preserve exactly the dihedral-angle information governing every rank-two subgroup. The assertion that these relations form a presentation removes the need to multiply explicit reflection matrices or reconstruct an entire tessellation in order to know the group: the finite Coxeter matrix determines \((W,S)\) up to isomorphism of Coxeter systems.

For the motivating classification problem, this reformulation supplied concrete tests and reductions. A disconnected diagram gives a direct product, so classification reduces to connected, irreducible diagrams. The standard bilinear form converts finiteness into positive definiteness; constraints on its principal submatrices then severely restrict possible connected diagrams. This yields the finite irreducible families and exceptional cases and simultaneously places regular-polytope reflection groups and Weyl groups within one classification [2][3]. The direct achievement was therefore not generic group notation: it was a finite combinatorial encoding whose relations were sufficient to recover the reflection group and whose associated form detected which encodings produced finite groups.

The deeper structural viewpoint is that the same data can be read in three equivalent ways: as orders of products of distinguished involutions, as angles between chamber walls, or as entries of a labeled diagram and its Gram-type form. That equivalence isolated the reflection mechanism itself from the coordinates of any one realization. Infinite Coxeter groups and their later applications extend this viewpoint, but they are not needed to explain the object's original role in the classification of discrete and finite reflection groups.

## 3. Notes

A Coxeter group is not synonymous with an arbitrary group generated by reflections. The term refers to the special presentation above; geometric reflection groups acquire Coxeter systems by choosing the reflections in the walls of a suitable fundamental chamber. Nor should a Coxeter diagram be confused with a Dynkin diagram: Dynkin diagrams retain crystallographic root-length information and orientations, whereas Coxeter diagrams record only the orders \(m_{st}\). Weyl groups form the crystallographic subclass of finite Coxeter groups; types such as \(H_3\), \(H_4\), and most \(I_2(m)\) are finite Coxeter groups but not Weyl groups of crystallographic root systems [3][4].
