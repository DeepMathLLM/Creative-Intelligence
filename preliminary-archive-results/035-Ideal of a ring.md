# Mathematical Object Origin Archive | Ideal of a Ring

## 1. Archive Information

- Standard Name: Ideal of a ring
- Mathematical Field: Algebraic Number Theory; Commutative Algebra
- Abstract: An ideal is an additive subgroup of a ring that is closed under multiplication by arbitrary ring elements. In its formative number-theoretic setting, the ring of integers of a number field, this concrete subset replaced Kummer's incompletely reified “ideal divisors” and restored a unique prime-factorization calculus at the level of ideals even when elements did not factor uniquely.

## 2. Core Record

### Precise Description

Let \(R\) be a commutative ring. An **ideal** \(I\) of \(R\) is a subset satisfying
\[
0\in I,\qquad x-y\in I\ \text{for all }x,y\in I,\qquad rx\in I\ \text{for all }r\in R,
x\in I.
\]
Equivalently, \(I\) is an additive subgroup of \(R\) stable under multiplication by every element of \(R\). The principal ideal generated by \(a\in R\) is
\[
(a)=aR=\{ar:r\in R\}.
\]
For ideals \(I,J\), their product is
\[
IJ=\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{n}x_k y_k:n\ge 0,\ x_k\in I,\ y_k\in J\right\}.
\]
A proper ideal \(\mathfrak p\) is prime if \(xy\in\mathfrak p\) implies \(x\in\mathfrak p\) or \(y\in\mathfrak p\). In the ring of integers \(\mathcal O_K\) of a number field \(K\), every nonzero proper ideal has a unique factorization
\[
I=\mathfrak p_1^{e_1}\cdots \mathfrak p_r^{e_r}
\]
into distinct nonzero prime ideals and positive integer exponents, uniquely up to ordering [1][3]. Thus it is ideals in this specific class of rings—not ideals in every commutative ring—that satisfy the factorization property central to the object's formation.

### Mathematical Context and Formation

The motivating problem class was to extend the arithmetic of divisibility and prime factorization from \(\mathbb Z\) to rings of algebraic integers \(\mathcal O_K\), as required when Diophantine equations are studied after adjoining algebraic numbers. The direct transfer of the fundamental theorem of arithmetic fails. For example, in \(\mathbb Z[\sqrt{-5}]\),
\[
6=2\cdot 3=(1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5}),
\]
and the four displayed nonunit factors are irreducible while the two factorizations are inequivalent. The norm \(N(a+b\sqrt{-5})=a^2+5b^2\) makes the obstruction explicit: there are no elements of norm \(2\) or \(3\), which rules out the possible nontrivial splittings of these factors. Elementwise primes therefore cannot provide a consistent inventory of the divisors needed for arithmetic in this ring.

Kummer's ideal divisors had already shown, first for important cyclotomic-integer settings, that one could recover unique factorization by formally adjoining prime divisors and specifying divisibility through congruence conditions. The inadequacy was not the divisibility behavior but the status and generality of the proposed divisors: they were not constructed as ordinary mathematical objects in the number field, and Kummer's machinery was tied to restricted settings [2][4]. Dedekind's decisive formation principle was to represent an ideal divisor by the totality of algebraic integers divisible by it. Such a totality is closed under subtraction and under multiplication by arbitrary algebraic integers, exactly the closure properties in the definition above. It is therefore a concrete subset of \(\mathcal O_K\), rather than a hypothetical extra number. Principal ideals \((a)\) retain ordinary elements inside this enlarged divisor calculus, while nonprincipal ideals supply the divisors that no element of the ring represents. Ideal multiplication then encodes composition of divisibility. This construction turned the desired laws of generalized divisors into operations and relations on definite subsets and allowed one theory to apply to the full rings of integers of arbitrary number fields [1][2].

### Essential Role

The ideal made the missing prime divisors tractable without forcing element factorization to become unique. Its defining absorption property packages all multiples of a generalized divisor, and inclusion reverses divisibility:
\[
I\mid J\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad J\subseteq I
\]
for ideals of \(\mathcal O_K\). Its product records simultaneous divisor multiplicities, and prime ideals supply the correct replacement for prime elements. In \(\mathbb Z[\sqrt{-5}]\), for instance, with
\[
\mathfrak p=(2,1+\sqrt{-5}),\quad
\mathfrak q=(3,1+\sqrt{-5}),\quad
\overline{\mathfrak q}=(3,1-\sqrt{-5}),
\]
one has
\[
(2)=\mathfrak p^2,\qquad (3)=\mathfrak q\overline{\mathfrak q},\qquad
(1+\sqrt{-5})=\mathfrak p\mathfrak q,\qquad
(1-\sqrt{-5})=\mathfrak p\overline{\mathfrak q}.
\]
Consequently, the two incompatible element factorizations of \(6\) induce the same prime-ideal factorization
\[
(6)=\mathfrak p^2\mathfrak q\overline{\mathfrak q}.
\]

More generally, unique factorization of nonzero ideals in \(\mathcal O_K\) assigns an unambiguous exponent \(v_{\mathfrak p}(I)\) to every nonzero prime ideal \(\mathfrak p\), so questions about divisibility become comparisons and additions of integer exponents. This is the precise part of ordinary arithmetic that survives. The construction does not erase the failure of elementwise unique factorization; rather, it explains that failure as the restriction of a sound factorization theory to principal ideals. That structural reformulation—moving from individual algebraic integers to multiplication-stable additive systems of them—is the ideal's direct contribution to the motivating problem, distinct from its later use as a general organizing object of ring theory.

## 3. Notes

The archive concerns Dedekind's set-theoretic ideal, not Kummer's earlier “ideal number” or “ideal divisor.” For a noncommutative ring one distinguishes left, right, and two-sided ideals; the two-sided definition requires both \(RI\subseteq I\) and \(IR\subseteq I\). Unique prime-ideal factorization holds for nonzero ideals of Dedekind domains, including rings of integers of number fields, but not for arbitrary commutative rings.
