# Mathematical Object Origin Archive | Second Hochschild Cohomology Group

## 1. Archive Information

- Standard Name: Second Hochschild cohomology group \(HH^2(A,M)\)
- Mathematical Field: Homological Algebra
- Abstract: For an associative \(k\)-algebra \(A\) and an \(A\)-bimodule \(M\), \(HH^2(A,M)\) is the quotient of associative multiplication defects by defects caused only by changing a linear section. It arose from the problem of classifying square-zero algebra extensions of \(A\) by \(M\) and deciding when such an extension contains a subalgebra mapping isomorphically onto \(A\). Its cocycle condition records associativity, while its coboundary relation removes the noncanonical choice of section.

## 2. Core Record

### Precise Description

Let \(k\) be a commutative ring, let \(A\) be an associative \(k\)-algebra, and let \(M\) be an \(A\)-bimodule. The Hochschild cochain module in degree \(n\) is
\[
C^n(A,M)=\operatorname{Hom}_k(A^{\otimes_k n},M).
\]
For \(f\in C^n(A,M)\), the Hochschild differential is
\[
\begin{aligned}
(\delta f)(a_1,\ldots,a_{n+1})={}&a_1f(a_2,\ldots,a_{n+1})\\
&+\sum_{i=1}^{n}(-1)^i f(a_1,\ldots,a_i a_{i+1},\ldots,a_{n+1})\\
&+(-1)^{n+1}f(a_1,\ldots,a_n)a_{n+1}.
\end{aligned}
\]
Since \(\delta^2=0\), the second Hochschild cohomology group is
\[
HH^2(A,M)=\ker\!\left(\delta:C^2(A,M)\to C^3(A,M)\right)
\big/
\operatorname{im}\!\left(\delta:C^1(A,M)\to C^2(A,M)\right).
\]
Thus a bilinear map \(\phi:A\otimes_k A\to M\) is a \(2\)-cocycle precisely when
\[
a\phi(b,c)-\phi(ab,c)+\phi(a,bc)-\phi(a,b)c=0,
\]
and a \(2\)-coboundary has the form
\[
(\delta g)(a,b)=a g(b)-g(ab)+g(a)b
\]
for some \(k\)-linear \(g:A\to M\) [1,2]. For unital algebras and unital bimodules, the normalized subcomplex—cochains vanishing whenever one argument is \(1\)—computes the same group and is convenient for unital extensions.

### Mathematical Context and Formation

The motivating problem is the classification and splitting of singular, or square-zero, algebra extensions
\[
0\longrightarrow M\longrightarrow E\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}A\longrightarrow 0,
\qquad M^2=0,
\]
that split as sequences of \(k\)-modules and induce a prescribed \(A\)-bimodule structure on \(M\). Two extensions are regarded as equivalent when an algebra isomorphism between their middle terms induces the identity on both \(M\) and \(A\). The concrete questions are: how can all such multiplication laws be classified without depending on arbitrary coordinates, and how can one decide whether \(p\) has an algebra section? Hochschild's original account connects the two-dimensional cohomology classes directly with singular extensions and identifies the zero class with the split case [1].

A \(k\)-linear section \(s:A\to E\) identifies the underlying \(k\)-module of \(E\) with \(A\oplus M\), but it need not preserve multiplication. Its failure is the factor set
\[
\phi(a,b)=s(a)s(b)-s(ab)\in M.
\]
Working only with \(s\) or with a chosen factor set is inadequate: neither is intrinsic, because replacing \(s\) by another linear section changes \(\phi\). Likewise, splitting the underlying module sequence says nothing by itself about whether an algebra section exists. The needed object must retain the associativity obstruction while discarding the section dependence.

The formation of \(HH^2(A,M)\) follows exactly from these two requirements. Associativity of multiplication in \(E\), together with \(M^2=0\), forces
\[
\delta\phi=0.
\]
If the section is changed to \(s'=s+g\), then
\[
\phi'=\phi+\delta g.
\]
Consequently the extension determines not a preferred factor set but a well-defined class \([\phi]\in HH^2(A,M)\). The cocycle equation and the quotient by coboundaries are therefore not formal decorations: they are respectively the algebra's associativity equation and the equivalence generated by changing the auxiliary linear splitting [1,2].

### Essential Role

The group makes the classification problem tractable by replacing varying extension algebras with computable equivalence classes of bilinear maps. Conversely, a Hochschild \(2\)-cocycle \(\phi\) defines a multiplication on \(A\oplus M\) by
\[
(a,m)(b,n)=\bigl(ab,\;an+mb+\phi(a,b)\bigr).
\]
This multiplication is associative exactly when \(\delta\phi=0\). Cocycles differing by a coboundary yield equivalent extensions, and every module-split square-zero extension with the fixed bimodule structure is obtained in this way. Hence there is a bijection between the relevant extension-equivalence classes and \(HH^2(A,M)\) [1,2].

The zero class answers the splitting question precisely. If \([\phi]=0\), changing the linear section by a suitable \(g\) removes the multiplication defect, producing an algebra section \(A\to E\); conversely, an algebra section gives \(\phi=0\). Thus \(HH^2\) overcomes the two original difficulties at once: it removes coordinate dependence and turns algebra splitting into the vanishing of a canonical class. Its deeper structural viewpoint is that an extension problem is controlled by a cochain complex: admissible structures are cocycles, changes of presentation are coboundaries, and genuine extension types are cohomology classes. In particular, the vanishing of \(HH^2(A,M)\) implies that every extension in this fixed class is equivalent to the semidirect square-zero extension \(A\ltimes M\); this is the direct role of the object, rather than a later application.

## 3. Notes

The classification stated above requires the extension to be split as a \(k\)-module sequence and its kernel to have zero multiplication. Over a field, module splitting is automatic for short exact sequences of vector spaces, but the algebra-splitting problem remains nontrivial. More general extensions with a non-square-zero kernel require additional data and are not classified by this single group in the same elementary manner. Hochschild cohomology in other degrees and its later role in deformation theory are related developments, but they are not the motivating object documented here.
